Case studies

Selected work, written for practitioners

Each study uses a natural experiment or quasi-random variation to separate cause from coincidence. That is the difference between "response times are longer where outcomes are worse" and "this delay causes these deaths".

Response times

How much does one minute of ambulance delay cost?

Health Economics 33(4), 2024 · paper
Problem
Response time is a core KPI for emergency services. How much does it actually matter for patients?
Data
Emergency calls and ambulance response times for cardiovascular events in an Italian region, linked to patient outcomes and weather.
Method
Rain during the mission slows ambulances for reasons unrelated to the patient's condition. I use it as a natural experiment (instrumental variables).
Result
One extra minute of average response time corresponds to about 105 additional deaths per year in the region. The paper also estimates the economic value of these lives.
So what
Cutting even one minute through vehicle positioning, dispatch speed or routing has a large, measurable return.
Dispatch · caller location

The hidden cost of not knowing where the caller is

Italian Economics Journal, 2025 · paper
Problem
Dispatchers often receive incomplete or imprecise addresses. How much time does this cost?
Data
Administrative records of the Liguria emergency medical system.
Method
Compare the outbound trip (destination uncertain) with the return trip (destination known), and private homes with public buildings, in a difference-in-differences design.
Result
Location uncertainty delays urgent missions by about 5 minutes, around 30% of travel time. The delay is about half as large on non-urgent missions.
So what
Better caller location (automatic handset location, address validation, dispatcher protocols) is one of the cheapest ways to save minutes.
Workforce · staffing models

Employees or contractors in the emergency department?

With D. Battiston and J. Blanes i Vidal · under review
Problem
Hospitals increasingly rely on contract doctors to fill shifts. Are they as productive as employees?
Data
Administrative records from the emergency department of a large public hospital.
Method
Patients are assigned to doctors almost at random, which allows a fair comparison.
Result
Contract doctors work about 35% slower and spend 8% more on tests and treatments, with no better outcomes. The gap comes from employment status, not from who is hired: employees do more unpaid overtime when it helps fellow employees.
So what
Staffing contracts shape team behaviour and productivity, not only cost.

Shift work and fatigue

Performance drops at the end of shifts. Comparing cases by urgency shows that fatigue, not slacking off, is an important driver. With P. Roberti, working paper.
