Projects

Some examples of projects I have developed

An emergency mission is a chain: call, dispatch, drive, treatment on scene, transport, handover at the hospital, patient care inside the emergency department, the ambulance's return to service. Performance targets look almost only at the first links. The projects below show that the decisive levers are often further down the chain, and in how people and rules are organised. For each project, one number, chart or table that captures what we learned.

The bigger picture

How an ambulance system really works, and what managers should watch

Measure the whole mission

Response time is necessary but not sufficient. Handover, return to service, destination choice and missions that end without transport decide how many ambulances are really available.

Rules only work if they are applied

One stroke patient in three goes to a different hospital from the one the rule indicates. Monitoring compliance comes before redesigning the network.

Capacity follows the rota, demand does not

Patients arrive smoothly over the day; capacity drops at every shift change. Staggered handovers protect the queue.

Who works matters as much as how many

Contract or employed doctors, volunteers or paid crews: employment models change productivity and resilience, not only cost.

Small frictions, large costs

An imprecise address costs about five minutes; one extra minute of response time costs lives. Logistical fixes upstream can have large returns.

Look after the people

Burnout and secondary trauma concentrate among clinical and experienced staff. Wellbeing is part of service performance.

Projects
Dispatch · caller location

The hidden cost of not knowing where the caller is

Italian Economic Journal, 2025 · paper

Dispatchers often receive imprecise addresses. Comparing the outbound trip (destination uncertain) with the return trip (destination known), on Liguria emergency data, isolates the time lost looking for the patient.

Extra driving time to reach a patient at home, by trip length (minutes)

Read the first row: whether the trip is under 5 km or over 20 km, finding a patient at home costs roughly 4 to 5 extra minutes. It is a fixed cost of locating the patient, not a matter of distance; for urgent missions it is about 5 minutes, +30% on driving time.

So whatBetter caller location (handset location, address validation, dispatcher protocols) is one of the cheapest ways to save minutes.

Hospital networks · rules vs. practice

Where do stroke patients go, and where should they go?

Lombardy, 2024 · 12,047 stroke patients transported by the 118 service

Lombardy has a rule for where ambulances should take stroke patients: the nearest hospital of the right level within 45 minutes' drive. The region is now reclassifying hospitals and considering a new rule. Before changing the rule, I checked whether the current one is followed. For every patient I estimated the drive time to each of 37 hospitals and computed where the rule would have sent them.

32.7%

of patients went to a different hospital from the one the current rule indicates (3,940 transports)

104vs 447

Milan Città Studi: patients actually received vs. patients the rule would have sent

So whatOne patient in three does not go where the rule says. Is it worth writing new rules, or changing them, if we do not follow the ones we have? Before redesigning the network, it pays to understand why practice departs from the rule.

Workforce · shifts

Emergency department productivity across the day

All emergency departments in Lombardy, 2019

Patients arrive at the emergency department following a smooth daily pattern. Doctors see them following the staff rota. Comparing the two, minute by minute across the whole region, shows where capacity is lost.

Each dot is one minute of the day, averaged over 2019 across all Lombardy emergency departments. Axis labels in Italian: pazienti in ingresso = arrivals, pazienti visitati = patients seen.

Demand peaks at about 14 arrivals per minute around 10:00, while doctors never see more than about 12. Instead of staying high to clear the queue, capacity collapses at the 14:00 and 20:00 shift changes. Overlapping shifts are not enough to keep the flow going.

So whatStaggering shift handovers and protecting capacity just after the morning peak would cut queues without hiring more doctors.

Workforce · staffing models

Employees or contractors in the emergency department?

Niguarda Hospital, Milan · with D. Battiston and J. Blanes i Vidal · under review

Hospitals increasingly fill emergency department shifts with contract doctors. Within each shift and treatment area, patients are assigned to doctors quasi-randomly, which allows a causal comparison of the two groups on the same kind of patients.

So whatThe gap comes from employment status, not from who is hired: employees put in extra effort for fellow employees, but not for contractors. Staffing contracts shape team behaviour and productivity, not only cost.

System resilience

Volunteers: the ambulance workforce that held during COVID

Lombardy ambulance services, 2018–2022 · work in progress

In Lombardy, ambulances are run by non-profit organisations, and employees and volunteers do exactly the same job on board: driver, crew leader, rescuer. Following every worker month by month shows how each group responded to the pandemic shock.

≈ −30%

employees: from about 30 to about 21 missions per month in spring 2020

≈ 6steady

volunteers: missions per month unchanged, even slightly up at the start of the pandemic

So whatVolunteers are a small but resilient share of capacity: when the system was under the greatest stress, their presence did not shrink. Emergency planning should count on them as a stabiliser, not only as extra hands.

Fleet capacity · hospital handover

The response-time KPI stops at the patient's door

With D. Battiston and J. Blanes i Vidal · Lombardy · about 7.3 million patients

Ambulance services are judged on response time. But once the ambulance reaches the emergency department, the crew must wait until the patient is taken over by hospital staff. Until then the vehicle is blocked and cannot answer the next call. In Lombardy this handover takes 21 minutes on average, and it varies hugely from one hospital to another.

21min

average handover time at the emergency department

9→38min

from the fastest to the slowest hospital

So whatEvery minute an ambulance waits at the hospital is a minute it is not available. Where handovers are slow, more vehicles and crews are needed to guarantee the same response times. Fleet sizing and hospital emergency department capacity must be planned together.

Demand · matching services to needs

One ambulance in nine does not take anyone to hospital

With D. Battiston and J. Blanes i Vidal · Lombardy, about 9.5 million patients

In 11% of missions the patient refuses transport to hospital. Was an ambulance the right response? The refusal rate depends heavily on the reason for the call: it is close to zero for time-critical emergencies, but very high for calls that are not really medical emergencies.

11%

of ambulance missions end with the patient refusing transport

≈ 0%

for time-critical conditions, amputations, childbirth

80–90%

for help getting someone back to bed, people stuck in lifts, assistance calls

So whatA large share of calls signal social and care needs rather than emergencies. Mapping who calls, for what and where shows which local services are missing, such as home assistance, lifting teams or community nursing, and frees ambulances for the patients who need them.

Dispatch centres · workload

What happens when a dispatch centre gets more calls?

With D. Battiston and J. Blanes i Vidal · Lombardy 2023 territorial restructuring · difference-in-differences

Lombardy's emergency calls are handled by four regional dispatch centres. In 2023 part of the Milan area was reassigned to the Laghi and Pianura centres, while the Alpina centre was unaffected. This sudden shift in workload works as a natural experiment: with Alpina as a control group, it is possible to measure how call pressure affects operators and the service, from calls per operator to call handling time, errors and the time for the ambulance to reach the patient.

−13%

incoming calls in Milan after the restructuring

+19%

incoming calls in Laghi (+8% in Pianura)

−6%

time from call to ambulance on scene in Milan; +2% in Pianura

So whatHow calls are split across dispatch centres is a management decision with measurable effects: lighter load means faster response, heavier load slows it. Redrawing territories should be planned together with staffing, and evaluated on the KPIs that matter to patients.
